\section*{Synopsis (100 words max)}

\textbf{Motivation:} Photogrammetric meshes of brain dissections could serve
as quantitative references for diffusion MRI, but they are open surfaces with
scanning defects.

\textbf{Goal(s):} Close them into watertight volumes and measure how far
these volumes can be trusted.

\textbf{Approach:} We repaired and closed all 130 meshes of the public
BraDiPho archive, compared closing methods and checked that volume decreases
along each dissection.

\textbf{Results:} Every mesh became watertight, and closing methods changed
volumes by 0.45\% on average. Yet volume increased in 15 of 104 dissection
steps, by up to 15\%, mostly because the scanned edge moved 2 to 5~mm
outward.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section*{Introduction}

Photogrammetry records a brain dissection as a series of textured 3D meshes,
one per step (``epoch'')$^{1,2}$. The BraDiPho archive$^{1}$ shares such
meshes to compare dissected pathways with diffusion MRI tractography, and
surface scans of dissected brains already support quantitative
measures$^{3}$. Measuring the volume removed at each step requires
watertight surfaces. However, each mesh is open on the face that was not
photographed and contains scanning defects (inverted faces, folds, floating
debris), and a watertight mesh is not necessarily a correct one (Figure 1).

We closed the whole archive and asked: (1) can the meshes be closed
automatically; (2) how much does the volume depend on how they are closed;
and (3) do the volumes behave as a dissection should? We report what works,
what does not, and what we wish we had known at the start.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section*{Methods}

\textbf{Data.} We used all 130 epochs of the 12 BraDiPho specimens$^{1}$.
Undocumented ``b'' variants were excluded from comparisons between epochs,
leaving 104 consecutive steps.

\textbf{Repair and closing} (Python, VTK; Figure 2). (1) Duplicated faces,
small flaps and small fragments are removed, non-manifold edges are split,
and faces are oriented outward. (2) Each opening, bounded by a loop, is
closed by a cap. A plane is fitted to the loop; if the loop projects onto it
without crossing itself (after straightening small folds of up to 20
vertices), a planar cap is triangulated$^{4}$ on the outer side of the
opening. Otherwise, a curved cap is built by harmonic interpolation.
(3) Remaining defects and debris are removed, and each part is oriented
outward. (4) Volume is computed only for watertight meshes.

\textbf{Evaluation.} Baselines: a simple fan closing of the same loops, two
variants of fold straightening (A: always planar; B: planar only if the cap
adds at most 5\% of volume) and MeshFix$^{5}$, an independent repair and
hole-filling method. We measured (a) watertightness; (b) the closing
uncertainty, i.e.\ the volume difference between methods; (c) visual defects
in every epoch; and (d) plausibility: dissection only removes tissue, so
volume should decrease. Each increase was compared with 7 normal steps for
scale (similarity ICP), displacement of the main cap and volume added by the
caps.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section*{Results}

\textbf{What works.} All 130 epochs became watertight (Figure 2).
Straightening small folds reduced the epochs needing a curved cap from 38 to
12. Volumes differed from the fan closing by 0.45\% on average (maximum
3.4\%) and from MeshFix by \todo{mean, max over 130 epochs; 0.73\% and
2.2\% on the first 21} (Figure 3c). After corrections, the epochs with
visual defects dropped from 29 to 12.

\textbf{What works poorly.} Most of these 12 epochs (7 from one specimen)
have truly folded openings, where the curved cap folds onto itself
(Figure 4a); others have small holes just under the planar cap (Figure 4b).
On four deeply hollowed openings, variants A and B differed by 5.7 to 11\%
in volume: there, the volume depends on an arbitrary choice.

\textbf{What does not work.} The median volume removed per step was 8.7\%
(range 0.5--37.5\%). Yet volume \emph{increased} in 15 of 104 steps, by up
to 15.3\%, and 21 changes were smaller than the closing uncertainty
(Figure 3a--b). Scale differences between epochs ($\leq$0.8\%) did not
explain the increases. In 9 of the 15, including the three largest, the
main cap lay 2.2 to 5.1~mm further out, adding 19 to 57~cm$^3$, whereas it
moved by $-2.2$ to $+1.9$~mm in normal steps (Figure 5). Two others followed
a poorly closed epoch, and four were within the closing uncertainty.

\section*{Discussion}

A watertight mesh is not necessarily correct: a three-vertex fold can warp a
planar cap, and inverted debris silently subtracts volume; only visual
inspection revealed them. The closing uncertainty (about 3\%, up to 11\%)
cannot explain increases of 13 to 15\%: the scanned edge itself moves, so
any closing method inherits the shift. Each millimetre changes the volume by
2 to 4\%, comparable to a typical dissection step. The limiting factor is the
acquisition, not the closing. We recommend photographing both faces at every
epoch, fixing the specimen on a rigid support with markers and a scale
reference, reprocessing the photographs with identical settings, and
reporting the closing uncertainty with every volume. Without reference
volumes, real tissue removal on the unphotographed face cannot yet be
separated from settling or movement of the specimen.
